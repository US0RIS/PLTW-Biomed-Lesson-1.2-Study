1.2.1

Anatomy of an Autopsy

An autopsy is a systematic examination of the entire body after death, done to find out how and why a person died. It is done when a death is suspicious or unexpected, when there's a public health concern (like a contagious disease), or when the family requests it. A medical examiner or coroner performs it.

External examinationBody is described (hair, eye color, sex), measured, and weighed. External injuries are noted.
IdentificationTattoos, birthmarks, and other marks. Personal documents (license) or friends/relatives.
DocumentationPhotographs record injuries, identifying features, and decomposition.
Internal examinationY incision from each shoulder to the center of the chest, then down the body. Organs are examined in place, removed, and weighed.
BrainChecked for injury, then removed from the skull, weighed, and examined.
Stomach contentsIdentity and degree of digestion of food or pills. Can help estimate time of death.
Sample collectionBlood, urine, and tissue samples are sent out for testing to help confirm identity and cause of death.
ToolsSaws, scissors, needles, and other tools for the internal exam and to stitch the body after.

Gross examination ("grossing") means looking at organs and tissues with the naked eye and describing them. It gives clues before microscopic work.

TermMeaningExample from the lesson
CauseThe specific injury, trauma, or disease that directly caused deathGunshot wound
MechanismWhat happens physiologically (inside the body) to result in deathExsanguination (extreme blood loss)
MannerThe circumstances: natural or unnatural. Unnatural is split into accident, homicide, or undetermined.Homicide

Manner usually needs the most information from outside the autopsy (scene evidence, history, witnesses).

Autopsy report sections (from the Autopsy Report Guide): decedent information, examination info (forensic vs. clinical autopsy), clothing and personal effects, medical intervention, features of identification, evidence of injury (sketched on anterior = front and posterior = back views, using the decedent's right and left), postmortem changes, external exam, internal exam, toxicology, lab, and histology results, case summary, and cause/mechanism/manner of death.

A forensic autopsy seeks cause and manner of death or identifies the decedent. A clinical (pathological) autopsy diagnoses disease or is done for research.

Body systems

Organs are grouped into systems that work together. A problem in one system can have minor to very serious effects on others.

1.2.2

Time of Death

Time of deathDefinition
PhysiologicalWhen the decedent's vital functions actually stopped. Hard to pin down exactly.
EstimatedThe time the medical examiner estimates the death occurred.
LegalThe time on the death certificate, based on when the body was found or pronounced dead.

Changes after death

ChangeTimingWhat happens
Algor mortis0–24 hBody cools (or warms) to match its surroundings, roughly 1.5 °F per hour. Reaches room temperature within about 24 hours. Room temperature, sun, wind, snow, and clothing all change the rate.
Corneal clouding3–4 hCorneas (normally clear) turn milky or cloudy within the first several hours.
Rigor mortisstarts 2–4 h
full 8–12 h
fades 36–48 h		Muscles contract and stiffen. Starts in the head and neck, then arms and legs. Full rigor "fixes" the body in its position at death. Fades as decomposition begins.
Livor mortis (lividity)early <2 h
full ~5 h
fixed 8–12 h		Heart stops, so gravity pulls blood to the parts of the body facing the ground. Shows as a bluish color.
Decomposition36–48 hBody turns greenish and may bloat as bacteria break down tissue and release gas. Also called putrefaction.
Insect activityvariesEggs, larvae, or adult insects show how long the body has been decaying. Blue bottle flies take about two weeks from egg to adult at 68–70 °F.

Investigators never rely on just one or two of these. Body clues are combined with scene markers: dated texts, emails, or mail, a broken clock, even dishes in the sink.

Glaister equation (uses °F): (98.4 − rectal temperature) ÷ 1.5 = approximate hours since death. Core temperature is taken rectally or with a probe in the liver or brain because of their large mass and density.

The cooling experiment

Problem statement: How do different ambient temperatures affect the rate of body cooling? Hot dogs stood in for bodies and a temperature probe tracked "body temperature."

1 Identify the question→2 Make a prediction→3 Design an experiment→4 Conduct the experiment→5 Analyze the data→6 Communicate findings
  • Hypothesis: a testable prediction. Independent variable: what you change (ambient temperature). Dependent variable: what you measure (body temperature over time).
  • Control group: a standard of comparison that does not receive the treatment, used as a benchmark to measure change in the group that did.
  • Celsius to Fahrenheit: (°C × 1.8) + 32 = °F. So 20 °C = 68 °F and 50 °C = 122 °F.

Body farm: research site where donated bodies decompose under different conditions. Dr. William Bass founded the first one in 1972 at the University of Tennessee, Knoxville. A forensic entomologist studies insects and arachnids in criminal investigations.

1.2.3

Forensic Toxicology

Toxicology studies the effects of chemicals (drugs, alcohol, poisons, metals, gases) on the body. "All things are poisons... It is only the dose which makes a thing poison." (Paracelsus). Safe dosage depends on height, weight, and other substances in the body.

A forensic toxicologist analyzes tissue and fluid samples from the autopsy for substances that may have contributed to death. Toxicologists also test living people, usually urine, blood, saliva, or hair.

Digestion and metabolism

  • The digestive system breaks food into nutrients and molecules used to build body parts, drive chemical reactions (like aerobic respiration), and supply energy. It also removes waste.
  • Physical digestion: mechanical breakdown, like chewing. Chemical digestion: acids and enzymes break food into smaller molecules (saliva enzymes turn complex carbohydrates into simple sugars).
  • Path of food: oral cavity → esophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine.
  • Metabolism: breaking substances down and converting the smaller molecules into new substances. Metabolites are the byproducts, found in blood, sweat, urine, skin, hair, and nails. Toxicologists test for metabolites of known substances.

Two kinds of tests

Presumptive testIs a type of substance present?Quick, usually a color change. Can't name the exact substance. Example: the Marquis test turns purple for opioids. Uses negative and positive controls.
Confirmatory testExactly what is it, and how much?Specific. Example: gas chromatography separates a compound's components by chemical properties (sample showed hydrocodone, acetaminophen, caffeine).

A forensic chemist tests nonbiological samples (powders, pills). The pill lab compared an unknown to aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid), acetaminophen, oxycodone (prescription opioid), and ecstasy (illicit), using color, texture, and reactions to distilled water, pH, hydrochloric acid, and ferric nitrate.

1.2.4

Histology

Cell→Tissue→Organ→Organ system→Organism

The nervous system (brain, brain stem, spinal cord, nerves) receives, interprets, and reacts to signals using chemical and electrical signals. It controls sensation, movement, digestion, and involuntary actions like breathing. Damage to the breathing area of the brain can stop breathing and cause death.

CNSCentral nervous systemBrain and spinal cord.
PNSPeripheral nervous systemAll the nerves in the body.
TBITraumatic brain injuryBrain dysfunction caused by an outside force to the head.
CTEChronic traumatic encephalopathyProgressive degeneration or death of nerve cells from repeated head injuries, like repeated concussions.

Four tissue types

TissueWhereJob
NervousNerves, spinal cord, brain. Made of neurons.Receive, interpret, and respond to signals
EpithelialSheets of connected cells lining outer surfaces of organs and blood vessels, the mouth, and skinAbsorb, secrete, protect, sense
MuscleSkeletal (striated, on bones), smooth (walls of internal organs), cardiac (heart walls)Contract
ConnectiveAttached to and between other tissuesSupport and connect: hold organs in place, attach muscle to bone, link bones at joints, let tissues stretch
  • A histologist examines tissues. Blunt force trauma, lack of oxygen, chemical exposure, and infection leave characteristic marks on tissue. Histologists also examine biopsies from living patients (for example, to confirm cancer).
  • All cells share the same genome (DNA). Different cell types come from different genes being used.
  • Total magnification = ocular (10×) × objective. 4× → 40×, 10× → 100×, 40× → 400×. Start on the 4× scanning objective, then move up.
1.2.5

Gross Anatomy Examination (heart)

The heart sits near the middle of the chest and pumps blood so nutrients are delivered, wastes are removed, and oxygen is carried from the lungs to the body. It has four chambers: two atria (upper) and two ventricles (lower), separated by valves.

ValveLocation / job
TricuspidBetween right atrium and right ventricle
PulmonaryLets blood flow from the right ventricle into the pulmonary artery
MitralBetween left atrium and left ventricle
AorticLets blood flow from the left ventricle into the aorta

Path of blood

Body→Vena cavae→Right atrium→Tricuspid→Right ventricle→Pulmonary valve→Pulmonary arteries→Lungs→Pulmonary veins→Left atrium→Mitral→Left ventricle→Aortic valve→Aorta→Body
  • Pulmonary circulation: heart → lungs to pick up oxygen → back to heart. Systemic circulation: oxygen-rich blood → body, deoxygenated blood → back to heart.
  • The exception to "arteries carry oxygenated blood": pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood to the lungs and pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood back to the heart.
  • The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it pumps blood to the whole body.
  • Anterior view = front. Posterior = back.

Conditions to know: sudden cardiac arrest, congenital heart failure, valve defects, atrial and ventricular septal defects (holes in the wall between chambers), Eisenmenger syndrome, bacterial endocarditis (infection of the heart lining/valves), and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (abnormally thick heart muscle). Left ventricular hypertrophy is thickening of the left ventricle wall. It can be fatal if untreated because a thick, stiff wall fills and pumps less effectively and can cause dangerous rhythms. Rare, but young healthy people can die suddenly from heart problems, often inherited ones.

1.2.6

Case Closed

Pull together scene evidence and the full autopsy report into a forensics report for the commissioner: evidence analyzed, autopsy report, timeline, conclusions, and a recommendation on whether to pursue a criminal case. Summarize Anna's cause, mechanism, and manner of death.

Errors on an autopsy report could lead to inaccurate findings, prosecution of the innocent, exoneration of the guilty, or false closure for families. "To the living we owe respect, but to the dead we owe only the truth." (Voltaire)

Confirmatory testExactly what is it, and how much?Specific. Example: gas chromatography separates a compound's components by chemical properties (sample showed hydrocodone, acetaminophen, caffeine).

A forensic chemist tests nonbiological samples (powders, pills). The pill lab compared an unknown to aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid), acetaminophen, oxycodone (prescription opioid), and ecstasy (illicit), using color, texture, and reactions to distilled water, pH, hydrochloric acid, and ferric nitrate.

Histology

Cell→Tissue→Organ→Organ system→Organism

The nervous system (brain, brain stem, spinal cord, nerves) receives, interprets, and reacts to signals using chemical and electrical signals. It controls sensation, movement, digestion, and involuntary actions like breathing. Damage to the breathing area of the brain can stop breathing and cause death.

CNSCentral nervous systemBrain and spinal cord.
PNSPeripheral nervous systemAll the nerves in the body.
TBITraumatic brain injuryBrain dysfunction caused by an outside force to the head.
CTEChronic traumatic encephalopathyProgressive degeneration or death of nerve cells from repeated head injuries, like repeated concussions.

TissueWhereJob

  • A histologist examines tissues. Blunt force trauma, lack of oxygen, chemical exposure, and infection leave characteristic marks on tissue. Histologists also examine biopsies from living patients (for example, to confirm cancer).
  • All cells share the same genome (DNA). Different cell types come from different genes being used.
  • Total magnification = ocular (10×) × objective. 4× → 40×, 10× → 100×, 40× → 400×. Start on the 4× scanning objective, then move up.
1.2.5

Gross Anatomy Examination (heart)

The heart sits near the middle of the chest and pumps blood so nutrients are delivered, wastes are removed, and oxygen is carried from the lungs to the body. It has four chambers: two atria (upper) and two ventricles (lower), separated by valves.

ValveLocation / job
TricuspidBetween right atrium and right ventricle
PulmonaryLets blood flow from the right ventricle into the pulmonary artery
MitralBetween left atrium and left ventricle
AorticLets blood flow from the left ventricle into the aorta

Path of blood

Body→Vena cavae→Right atrium→Tricuspid→Right ventricle→Pulmonary valve→Pulmonary arteries→Lungs→Pulmonary veins→Left atrium→Mitral→Left ventricle→Aortic valve→Aorta→Body
  • Pulmonary circulation: heart → lungs to pick up oxygen → back to heart. Systemic circulation: oxygen-rich blood → body, deoxygenated blood → back to heart.
  • The exception to "arteries carry oxygenated blood": pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood to the lungs and pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood back to the heart.
  • The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it pumps blood to the whole body.
  • Anterior view = front. Posterior = back.

Conditions to know: sudden cardiac arrest, congenital heart failure, valve defects, atrial and ventricular septal defects (holes in the wall between chambers), Eisenmenger syndrome, bacterial endocarditis (infection of the heart lining/valves), and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (abnormally thick heart muscle). Left ventricular hypertrophy is thickening of the left ventricle wall. It can be fatal if untreated because a thick, stiff wall fills and pumps less effectively and can cause dangerous rhythms. Rare, but young healthy people can die suddenly from heart problems, often inherited ones.
